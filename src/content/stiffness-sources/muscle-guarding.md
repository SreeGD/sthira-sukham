Guarding is a protective reflex. When the nervous system reads a joint as vulnerable, it
raises the resting tension in the muscles around it, restricting the range before anything
can reach the end of it. It is a genuinely useful response to an acutely injured joint.

It becomes a problem when it outlasts what caused it. The knee is protected from a threat
that has resolved, and the reduced movement itself becomes the ongoing source of the
stiffness.

Guarding has a recognisable quality: it eases as you warm up, varies a lot between days,
and often feels different at the end of a range than a true tissue restriction does — less
of a firm wall and more of a reluctance. It also tends to respond to slow, unhurried,
predictable movement, which is one reason movement traditions built around exactly that
show up in this library.
