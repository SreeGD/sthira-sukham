The pattern most often described in people with no injury and no diagnosis. Long hours
seated hold the hip and knee at roughly ninety degrees, and the tissues adapt to that as
their working range. Both ends of the range are given up gradually enough that the loss is
not noticed until something demands them — kneeling, squatting, or getting up after a long
drive.

Two structures do most of the work, and it is worth noting that neither is really about the
knee. The hip flexors shorten at the front of the hip; the calf shortens at the back of the
ankle. Both change how the knee sits and moves, which is why so much of the movement
material in this library addresses the hip and the ankle rather than the knee itself.

Of the four patterns described here, this is the most responsive to simply moving through a
fuller range regularly.
