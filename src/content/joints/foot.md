The foot has a harder problem than any other joint in this reference, because it has to be two
opposite things within a single second.

When the heel lands, the foot must be **soft** — spreading, rolling slightly inward, letting the
arch flatten to absorb the impact. When the heel lifts, the same foot must be **stiff** — a rigid
lever you can drive the whole body forward over. Softness at push-off wastes the effort. Stiffness
at heel strike sends the shock upward, and the knee is next in line.

### The windlass: how the foot stiffens itself

The mechanism is worth knowing because it explains why the big toe matters so much more than its
size suggests.

The plantar fascia runs from the heel forward, and wraps around the base of the toes. As the heel
lifts and **the big toe bends upward**, that band winds around the toe joint like a rope around a
drum — pulling the heel and the ball of the foot towards each other, and drawing the arch up into
a rigid arc. The foot stiffens itself, passively, with no muscular effort at all.

This is called the windlass mechanism, and it fails from either end. If the big toe cannot bend
upward far enough, the rope never winds and the arch never locks. If the fascia has lost its
elasticity, winding it achieves less.

### Why the big toe is the one to check

Walking needs roughly 65° of upward bend at the big toe joint, and it needs it on every single
step. That is a great deal of motion from a small joint, and losing it is consequential in a way
that losing motion at the smaller toes is not.

When the big toe will not extend, the body finds the range elsewhere. The most common substitution
is to roll off the *inside* of the foot instead of over the toe, which turns the foot inward and
changes what the knee above it has to tolerate — the same chain logic that runs through the rest
of this reference, one joint further down.

### What is not covered here

The ankle joints — the hinge for up-and-down and the joint below it for side-to-side tilt — are
covered on the ankle page. This page is about what happens in front of them: the arch, the
midfoot, and the toes.
