The ankle is two joints doing two different jobs, and confusing them is the most common mistake
people make about it.

The **talocrural joint** is the hinge between the shin bones and the talus. It handles
dorsiflexion — the shin travelling forward over the foot — and plantarflexion, pointing the toes.
The **subtalar joint** sits just below it and handles the side-to-side tilt of the foot, which is
what lets you stand on uneven ground without falling over.

**Dorsiflexion is the direction that matters functionally**, and it is the one most often lost.
Nearly every demanding lower-limb movement requires the shin to travel forward over a planted
foot: walking downhill, descending stairs, squatting, lunging, standing up from low seats. When
that motion is not available, it does not simply stop happening — the body takes it from
somewhere else, usually by letting the heel lift, the foot roll inward, or the knee travel on an
unusual path.

### Why bent-knee and straight-knee dorsiflexion differ

This is the single most useful thing to understand about the ankle.

Two muscles make up the calf. **Gastrocnemius** originates *above* the knee, on the thigh bone, so
it is stretched when the knee is straight and slack when it is bent. **Soleus** lies beneath it and
runs only from the shin bones to the heel, so the knee makes no difference to it at all.

Test dorsiflexion with the knee straight and you are measuring the shorter of the two. Test it with
the knee bent and gastrocnemius is out of the picture, leaving soleus alone. Most people have
noticeably more range in one than the other, and which one is short determines what is worth
working on.

It also determines which positions cause trouble. Squatting, sitting down and going downstairs are
bent-knee positions — so they are soleus's territory, not gastrocnemius's, which is why people who
diligently stretch their calves with a straight leg often see no change in any of them.
