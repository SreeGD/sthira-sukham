Sartorius is the longest muscle in the body — a thin strap running diagonally from the bony
point at the front of the pelvis, across the thigh, to the inner side of the knee.

Its name comes from *sartor*, Latin for tailor, after the cross-legged position tailors
worked in. That is a genuinely useful mnemonic, because the position names every action the
muscle has at once: **the hip bends, moves out to the side and turns outward, while the knee
bends.** If sitting cross-legged is hard, this is one of the muscles involved.

It belongs in a stiffness reference for a reason that is easy to miss. Most structures here
restrict one thing. Sartorius crosses two joints and produces a combination — a hip that
resists both straightening *and* turning inward. That pairing gets routinely attributed to
the hip flexors alone, which leads to stretching that addresses part of the problem.

At the knee end it joins two other muscles at a shared attachment on the inner side, just
below the joint. That spot is a common site of tenderness, and when it is sore, all three
muscles are usually implicated together rather than one of them individually.

Its size sets expectations: it is thin, and it is not a prime mover for anything. It rarely
needs strengthening on its own. It is worth knowing about mainly because of the pattern it
explains.
