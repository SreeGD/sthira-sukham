  - label: Alternate slowly
    detail: Alternate between the two, taking two or three seconds each way. Speed defeats it.
  - label: Stop while it is still accurate
    detail: When the toes stop separating and the whole foot starts lifting instead, the set is

Almost nobody can do this the first time, and that is the point of including it.

Pilates treats the foot the way it treats the spine — as something to move one segment at a time
rather than as a single block. Asking the big toe to lift while the others stay down is a control
task, not a strength or flexibility one, and the difficulty is a fair measure of how much
independent control the foot has left.

**Do not chase range.** A toe that lifts a millimetre on its own is a success. A whole forefoot
lifting because the intended toe would not is the substitution you are trying to eliminate, and
practising it makes it stronger.

Working both feet together is deliberate. Most people have one foot that is noticeably better at
this, and feeling the two side by side teaches the harder one what is being asked more effectively
than concentrating on it alone.

The visible tendons on the top of the foot are worth watching while you do it. If they stand up
hard and cordy every time, the long muscles are doing work the small ones inside the foot should
be sharing.
