---
name: Controlled ankle tilting with a band
modality: clinical-rom
startPosition: seated
targets:
  - peroneals
  - tibialis-posterior
  - tibialis-anterior
goal:
  - strength
  - motor-control
quickSteps:
  - Sit with the leg out, band round the forefoot.
  - Anchor the band to one side.
  - Turn the sole slowly against the band.
  - Return slowly. Do not let it snap back.
keyPoint: The return is the exercise. Letting the band pull the foot back skips the half that matters.
feelItIn: The outer or inner side of the lower leg, depending on the direction.
commonMistake: Turning the whole leg from the hip instead of tilting the foot at the ankle.
instructions:
  - label: Get into position
    detail: Sit with the leg you are working on extended — start with the stiffer ankle if one is
      worse and loop a resistance band around the ball of the foot.
  - label: Anchor the band
    detail: Anchor the free end to one side — to a table leg, or held by the opposite hand — so the
      pull is sideways.
  - label: Turn against the band
    detail: Slowly turn the sole of the foot away from the anchor, moving only at the ankle, and
      hold briefly at the end.
  - label: Return under control
    detail: Let the foot come back slowly against the band's pull rather than allowing it to snap
      back. Complete both directions on this side, then swap and repeat with the other foot.
dosage:
  reps: 10 to 15 repetitions each direction per side
  sets: 1 to 3 sets per session
  frequency: Once daily
  note: Commonly described as standard ankle strengthening after sprain.
difficulty: intermediate
regressions:
  - ankle-alphabet
progressions:
  - single-leg-balance
contraindications:
  - Acute ankle sprain within the first days, unless a clinician has cleared movement
  - Recent ankle fracture or surgery without clearance
stopIf:
  - Sharp pain at the outer or inner ankle
  - The ankle feels like it wants to give way
  - Swelling increases afterwards
equipment:
  - resistance-band
  - chair
evidenceLabel: mechanistic-rationale
sources:
  - neumann-kinesiology-2016
laterality: unilateral
---

The muscles on the outer side of the lower leg are the ankle's brakes against rolling over, and
they are the structure most consistently left impaired after a sprain.

What is usually lost is speed rather than raw strength — the muscle responds a fraction late,
which is enough for the ankle to have gone over before the correction arrives. Loaded work like
this rebuilds capacity; balance work rebuilds the timing, and both are needed.

Turning the foot outward against the band trains the peroneals; turning it inward trains
tibialis posterior, which holds up the arch. Doing both keeps the two sides in proportion.
