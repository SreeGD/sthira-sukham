name: Rising and sinking
traditionalName: Qi Shi (opening movement)
tradition: Qigong and tai chi (the opening of most forms)
modality: taichi-qigong
startPosition: standing
targets: [rectus-femoris, vastus-intermedius, soleus, gluteus-maximus, popliteus]
goal: [mobility, motor-control]
quickSteps:
  - Stand, knees soft, arms hanging.
  - 'Breathe in: straighten, arms float up.'
  - 'Breathe out: sink, arms settle down.'
  - Keep it continuous.
keyPoint: >-
  Tie it to the breath. That sets a pace slow enough to matter.
feelItIn: >-
  The knee moving freely through a small range.
commonMistake: >-
  Speeding up, so momentum carries the movement.
instructions:
  - Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, knees soft, arms hanging.
  - As you breathe in, straighten the legs slowly and let the arms float up in front of you to about shoulder height.
  - As you breathe out, sink by bending the knees again and let the arms settle back down.
  - Keep the movement continuous and tie it to the breath. Neither end is a stopping point.
dosage: Commonly practised as 6 to 12 cycles, or a few minutes of continuous movement.
difficulty: beginner

contraindications:
  - Knee pain on repeated partial squatting
  - Balance problems without support within reach
  - Dizziness on repeated arm raising
stopIf:
  - Knee pain during the sinking phase
  - Light-headedness
  - Any catching or locking as the knee straightens
equipment: [none]

The opening movement of most tai chi forms, and a good first thing to do with a knee that
has been still.

What it offers a stiff joint is repetition through a small range with no end-range demand at
all. The knee passes through the transition out of straight and back again, over and over,
without ever being pushed. That transition is where popliteus does its work and where many
people feel the first catch of the day.

Tying it to the breath is not decoration — it sets a pace slow enough that momentum cannot
carry the movement, which is the same principle behind everything else in this modality.
