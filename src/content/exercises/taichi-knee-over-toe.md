---
name: Knee-over-toe tracking
traditionalName: Knee alignment in stance work
tradition: Tai chi (a principle applied throughout the forms)
modality: taichi-qigong
startPosition: standing
targets:
  - vastus-medialis
  - gluteus-medius
  - gluteus-minimus
goal:
  - motor-control
quickSteps:
  - Stand where you can see your knees.
  - Bend both knees slowly, a short way.
  - Watch each knee track over the middle of the foot.
  - Straighten slowly, same tracking.
keyPoint: Watch. This fault is invisible from the inside.
feelItIn: The thigh working, with the knee staying in line.
commonMistake: Letting the knee drift inward, usually without noticing.
instructions:
  - label: Find a mirror
    detail: Stand with feet hip-width apart, near a mirror or somewhere you can see your knees.
  - label: Bend slowly
    detail: Bend both knees slowly a short way, watching that each knee travels forward over the
      middle of the foot rather than drifting inward.
  - label: Check the tracking
    detail: Hold the bent position briefly and check the alignment again — this is where it usually
      slips.
  - label: Straighten slowly
    detail: Straighten slowly, keeping the same tracking on the way up.
dosage:
  reps: 8 to 12 slow cycles
  sets: 1 to 2 sets per session
  frequency: Once daily
  note: Commonly practised as 10 to 15 slow repetitions, with attention on alignment rather than
    depth.
difficulty: beginner
regressions:
  - taichi-weight-shifting
progressions:
  - taichi-bow-stance
contraindications:
  - Knee pain during a partial squat
  - Recent knee surgery without clearance
stopIf:
  - Front-of-knee pain
  - You cannot keep the knee tracking correctly even in a very small range — that is worth
    showing to a physiotherapist rather than repeating
equipment:
  - none
evidenceLabel: mechanistic-rationale
sources:
  - wang-taichi-knee-oa-2016
  - neumann-kinesiology-2016

Tai chi treats knee alignment as a principle running through every posture rather than as a
separate exercise, and pulling it out to practise deliberately is a reasonable adaptation for
someone working on a specific knee.

The knee drifting inward as it bends is one of the most common movement faults associated with
front-of-knee problems, and it is rarely a knee problem. The femur is rotating from above,
usually because the hip is not controlling it. What you are training here is the hip, using the
knee as the visible indicator.

Watching matters more than usual. This fault is largely invisible from the inside — the
movement does not feel wrong — so a mirror or a phone propped against something does more for
this exercise than any amount of concentration.
